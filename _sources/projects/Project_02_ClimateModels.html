Climate Model Neural Network Prediction#

Background#

Climate change is one of the most important problems facing humanity in the years ahead, with numerous predicted regional or global changes, such as:

  • rising sea level [1];

  • increases in fire activity, fire risk, and burned area [2, 3, 4];

  • likely increases in hurricane intensity [5];

  • changes in the global atmospheric and oceanic circulation [6], with potentially severe impacts. For example, a collapse of the Atlantic overturning circulation in model experiments leads to strong cooling over the North Atlantic and Western Europe [7], although how much the circulation will actually weaken is uncertain [8].

Some of the most important tools used by climate scientists are computationally expensive numerical fluid-dynamics simulations called general circulation models (GCMs). They are used to predict and understand historical, future, or idealized climate scenarios: for example, an instantaneous quadrupling of \(CO_2\) [9], “aquaplanet” simulations of an Earth covered entirely by ocean [10], or “hosing” experiments, in which a large input of freshwater from melting ice is added to the ocean [7]. The Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP) coordinates a standard set of such simulations across the world’s modelling centres.

There are dozens of high-quality GCMs developed around the world. Four leading models in the United States are:

  • the Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory (GFDL) model, developed by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) in collaboration with Princeton University;

  • the NASA Goddard Institute for Space Studies (GISS) model, developed with Columbia University;

  • the Department of Energy’s Energy Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM);

  • the Community Earth System Model (CESM) at the National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR).

Each modelling centre develops its own model, with different physical representations of processes, and together their simulations span much of the uncertainty in our understanding of the Earth system. Half of the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Syukuro Manabe and Klaus Hasselmann, pioneers of climate modelling, “for the physical modelling of Earth’s climate, quantifying variability and reliably predicting global warming”. The next phase of CMIP, CMIP7, is under way, with all the major modelling centres producing new simulations with improved models and new experiments.

  1. learning to work with spatio-temporal climate-model output;

  2. working with a small ensemble of climate models; and

Data Sources#

We now start working with model output. Climate-model output is extremely large, so it is common to select a subset by time, variable, or location. In addition, there are dozens of models, several simulations for each model, and different versions of the same model as it improves over time. Working with these “super-ensembles” is not feasible without high-performance computing (HPC) clusters. Fortunately, the community makes the simulation output relatively easy to access with limited resources, via the ESGF portal.

Downloading data#

wget scripts are the usual way to download climate-model output from ESGF; Globus is an alternative for large transfers. For this project we download two files directly. Both are historical simulations from CMIP3 [12], an earlier phase of the model intercomparison project:

  • GFDL CM2.0, historical simulation, monthly near-surface air temperature tas, January 1861 – December 2000 (87 MB) [13]

  • NCAR CCSM3.0, historical simulation, January 1870 – December 1999 (13 files of 16 MB, combined into one) [14]

Historical simulations reproduce the climate of the past ~150 years, driven by the historical changes in greenhouse gases and other forcings, such as aerosols and solar output (the forcings included differ somewhat between models). We first download the GFDL CM2.0 output.

final_filename = "gfdl_cm2_1861_2000_historical_surfacetemperature.nc"

def verify_file(filepath, expected_hash):
    if not os.path.exists(filepath):
        return False
    sha256 = hashlib.sha256()
    with open(filepath, "rb") as f:
        for chunk in iter(lambda: f.read(4096), b""):
            sha256.update(chunk)
    return sha256.hexdigest() == expected_hash

if verify_file(final_filename, expected_hash) or verify_file(original_filename, expected_hash):
    print(f"File is already downloaded and verified.")
else:
    print(f"Downloading GFDL dataset...")
    urllib.request.urlretrieve(url, original_filename)
    
    # Verify the fresh download
    if verify_file(original_filename, expected_hash):
        print("Download complete and SHA256 verified!")
    else:
        print("Warning: File downloaded, but checksum failed.")

if os.path.exists(original_filename) and not os.path.exists(final_filename):
    os.rename(original_filename, final_filename)
    print(f"Renamed to: {final_filename}")

print("\nDone! Ready for analysis.")

Analyzing Climate Model Simulation (GFDL_CM2.0)#

After downloading the data we can subset and use some python libraries to help with the plotting.

# Here we open_dataset using xarray, then take the time mean
gfdl_hist = xr.open_dataset('gfdl_cm2_1861_2000_historical_surfacetemperature.nc')
# we can also subtract out 273.15 to make the Kelvin to Celsius conversion
gfdl_hist['tas'] = gfdl_hist['tas'] - 273.15
time_mean_gfdl_hist = gfdl_hist.mean("time")

time_mean_gfdl_hist.head()

time_mean_gfdl_hist is now a multidimensional xarray dataset, with lon and lat defining the spatial dimensions and tas giving the mean surface temperature values over the entire time domain. Xarray is a standard tool for working with climate data because it provides convenient built-in functionality for labeled multidimensional arrays. It draws on functionality from pandas and NumPy, and also supports more advanced workflows such as chunking and distributed computing.
Now we can plot the time mean over space with the below code:
